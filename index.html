The history of women in Mexico spans from pre-Columbian societies through Spanish colonization and into the modern era. In indigenous civilizations like the Aztecs and Maya, women managed households, arranged marriages, and held vital economic roles in local markets, though they generally lacked land ownership. The 16th-century Spanish conquest introduced a rigid casta system and Catholic traditions that restricted female independence. Despite these constraints, figures like the interpreter La Malinche shaped the colonial encounter, while intellectuals like the 17th-century nun Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz carved out profound spaces for female scholarship, writing, and early education.

During the 1910 Mexican Revolution, women stepped onto the national stage as soldaderas, serving as nurses, logisticians, and armed combatants who fought alongside male soldiers. This period of upheaval catalyzed an organized feminist movement, leading to landmark gatherings like the First Feminist Congress in Yucatán in 1916, which demanded legal and educational equality. Decades of grassroots activism eventually secured major legal milestones, including divorce and property reforms and, crucially, national women's suffrage in 1953. Cultural icons like Frida Kahlo further challenged traditional gender norms through art and politics during the mid-20th century.
